# Competitive Analysis
Competitive analysis maps what customers can use instead of your product, so that positioning, pricing and priorities rest on the real landscape. Most founders look only at direct competitors; indirect competitors and "doing nothing" are often the bigger threat.

## The four types of competition
- Direct competitors: solve the same problem in a similar way (for a project management tool, other project management tools)
- Indirect competitors: solve the same problem in a different way (a shared spreadsheet, an email thread)
- Potential competitors: companies that could enter the space by adding a feature to a product customers already use
- Substitutes: non-product alternatives, such as a daily meeting, a paper notebook, or doing nothing

## SWOT analysis
SWOT describes a competitor, or your own product, in four lists.
- Strengths: what it does well (features, brand, distribution, loyalty, pricing power, technology)
- Weaknesses: where it is vulnerable (product gaps, poor experience, slow innovation, high price, weak support, legacy technology)
- Opportunities: market conditions that favor a newcomer (changing needs, new technology, regulation, underserved segments, new channels)
- Threats: what could block success (competitor resources, switching costs, data advantages, patents, brand loyalty)
Strengths and weaknesses are internal to the company; opportunities and threats belong to the market.

## The competitive positioning matrix
Place every competitor on two axes that matter to customers in this market, for example simple ↔ powerful and individual ↔ team.
- Empty quadrants are possible white space, or places where nobody wants to be
- Crowded quadrants show where positioning overlaps
- The axes must be dimensions customers choose on, not dimensions that flatter your product

## The feature comparison matrix
A table of key features against each competitor shows gaps and overlaps. Use it to find where to differ, not to chase parity: differentiation beats completeness, and "me too" features rarely move a customer.

## The Jobs-to-be-Done competitive map
Instead of comparing features, rate each alternative from 1 to 5 on how well it does the customer's most important jobs. Include substitutes and "doing nothing" as columns.
- Jobs where every alternative scores low are underserved: that is where a new product can win
- "Doing nothing" always scores highest on low friction and low cost
- A new product has to be 10x better on the jobs that matter most to overcome that

## Porter's Five Forces
Five forces describe how attractive a market is.
- Threat of new entrants: high when barriers are low and there are no network effects
- Bargaining power of suppliers: high when suppliers are few or inputs are critical
- Bargaining power of buyers: high when buyers are few, switching is cheap and alternatives are many
- Threat of substitutes: high when customers can reach the same outcome another way
- Industry rivalry: high when competitors are many, growth is slow and products look alike

## Competitive advantages (moats)
A moat is what keeps a product defensible over time.
- Network effects: value rises as more people join (marketplaces, communication tools)
- Economies of scale: unit cost falls as volume grows
- Switching costs: leaving is expensive because of integrations, data or habits
- Brand: trust and emotional connection
- Proprietary technology: unique algorithms, patents or data
- Regulatory advantage: licenses or compliance that others lack
A product without a moat needs a plan to build one, or competitors will commoditize it.

## The four differentiation wedges
A wedge is how a new product first wins against established ones.
- Better on one key dimension: dramatically better at the thing that matters most
- Serve an underserved segment: a niche incumbents ignore
- Different business model: change how the value is paid for
- Radical simplicity: remove complexity incumbents are attached to

## Red flags in a competitive landscape
- A crowded market with no clear leader suggests commoditization
- A dominant player with most of the market is hard to dislodge
- High churn across all players suggests nobody has solved the problem well
- A mature, slow-growing market limits upside

## What to find out about each competitor
- Who they are: size, funding, background
- What they offer: features, pricing, positioning
- Who they serve and how they reach them
- What they do better than anyone, and where they are vulnerable
These are facts to research, not to assume. An analysis is only as good as the facts fed into it.
