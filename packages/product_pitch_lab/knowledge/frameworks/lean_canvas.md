# Lean Canvas
The Lean Canvas is a one-page business model template created by Ash Maurya, adapted from the Business Model Canvas for early-stage products. It has nine blocks, filled in the order below. Every entry is a hypothesis to test, not a fact. It is a thinking tool for systematic experimentation, not a business plan.

## Lean Canvas block 1: Problem
List the top three problems the target customer faces.
- Describe real, observable pain points, not assumed needs
- Rank them by severity and frequency
- Note the existing alternatives: what customers do today, including "nothing"

## Lean Canvas block 2: Customer Segments
Define who has the problem.
- Start with a narrow, specific group rather than a broad market
- Name the early adopters: the people who feel the pain most and will try an unfinished product
- Describe them by behavior and need, not only demographics
- Example: "Freelance designers who invoice more than five clients a month"

## Lean Canvas block 3: Unique Value Proposition
A single clear statement of why this product deserves attention.
- It answers "why you, why now?"
- It names an outcome for the customer, not a feature
- It is specific and differentiated from the existing alternatives
- Template: "We help [customer] achieve [benefit] by [unique approach]"

## Lean Canvas block 4: Solution
The smallest set of features that addresses the problems.
- One feature per problem listed in block 1, three at most
- Think minimum viable product: what is the least that proves the idea?
- A solution that does not map to a listed problem does not belong on the canvas

## Lean Canvas block 5: Channels
How customers will hear about the product and get it.
- Distinguish free channels (content, community, referrals) from paid ones (ads, partnerships)
- Distinguish direct channels (own site, sales) from indirect ones (marketplaces, resellers)
- Pick one or two to test first; a channel is a hypothesis too

## Lean Canvas block 6: Revenue Streams
How the product makes money.
- Name the pricing model: subscription, one-time purchase, freemium, usage-based
- Name who pays; it is not always the user
- Revenue = number of customers × price × purchase frequency
- A price is an assumption until someone has paid it

## Lean Canvas block 7: Cost Structure
What it costs to build and run.
- Fixed costs: salaries, infrastructure, rent
- Variable costs: anything that grows with each customer or transaction
- Identify the biggest cost drivers; they determine burn rate and runway

## Lean Canvas block 8: Key Metrics
The one to three numbers that show whether the business is working.
- Choose metrics that drive decisions: activation rate, retention, churn, customer acquisition cost, recurring revenue
- Avoid vanity metrics such as page views, downloads or sign-ups that never convert

## Lean Canvas block 9: Unfair Advantage
Something that cannot be easily copied or bought.
- Examples: network effects, insider knowledge, an existing audience, a rare team, proprietary data
- This is the hardest block to fill, and leaving it empty early on is honest
- "First mover", "passion" and "better features" are not unfair advantages

## Using the Lean Canvas well
- Problem and Customer Segments carry the riskiest assumptions; fill and test them first
- Keep it to one page: the limit forces prioritization
- For every block, ask what must be true for it to hold, then design the cheapest test of that
- Revise the canvas as evidence arrives; a canvas that never changes is not being used

## Common Lean Canvas mistakes
- Customer segments so broad that nobody in particular is described
- Listing features where problems should be
- Claiming an unfair advantage that a competitor could copy in months
- Writing revenue projections that no customer conversation supports
- Treating the canvas as a finished document rather than a set of open hypotheses
