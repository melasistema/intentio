# Product Mockup Principles
A product mockup shows a concept before it is built, so that customers, investors and collaborators can react to it. It is a communication tool, not a work of art. The priority order is clarity (what is it?), value (why does it matter?), then aesthetics.

## The four core principles of a mockup
- Clarity over realism: the viewer should understand what the product is and does at a glance; excess detail obscures purpose
- One key feature: use contrast, a glow or a closer view to draw the eye to what makes the product different
- Visual hierarchy: the product first, its differentiating feature second, supporting context third, background last
- One consistent style: choose a single aesthetic (modern, playful, professional) and hold it

## Form and silhouette of physical products
- A clean shape that is recognizable from the chosen angle
- No visual clutter
- Ergonomics should read at a glance: it should look comfortable to hold or use

## Proportion and scale
The size of a product follows from what it holds, or from the hand that uses it. Work this out before describing the shape.
- A container is only a little larger than its content; unneeded volume reads as clumsy
- A handheld tool is sized to the grip: palm-sized, pen-sized, the length of a forearm
- Name the proportions in words: "low and slim", "tall and narrow", "flat and wide"
- Compare the size with a familiar object, because an image model has no ruler: "a little longer than a pair of glasses, as thick as a paperback book"
- Always give the thickness or height as well as the footprint; an image model told only the footprint draws a tall object

## Showing what the product does
A shape alone rarely says what a product is for. Add one cue of its function.
- A product that holds something is shown holding it, and a closed lid hides the content: write "the lid half open, showing the glasses lying inside"
- A tool is shown with what it works on: a pen beside the line it has drawn
- One cue is enough; a second one competes with the product

## Design language of a contemporary product
A product reads as modern and desirable when little is left to remove.
- Few parts: one body, one lid, one control
- Continuous surfaces, softly rounded corners, tight seams
- One main material, and at most one accent material or color
- Controls are plain forms set flush with the surface: a ring, a slider, a single button
- A control shows its setting by its position or by a light, with nothing printed around it
- Feedback through one small light or a thin line of light, never a screen unless the screen is the product
- Ornament is left out: plain surfaces carry no engraving, numerals or printed scales

## Communicating materials
The material tells the viewer how the product is positioned.
- Metal: reflective or brushed surfaces, cool tones. Reads as premium and durable.
- Plastic: matte or glossy finish, brighter colors. Reads as accessible and light.
- Glass: transparency and refraction. Reads as refined.
- Wood: visible grain, warm tones. Reads as natural and crafted.
- Fabric and leather: soft texture, stitching. Reads as comfortable and personal.

## Color strategy for products
- A neutral base (grays, black, white) conveys professionalism
- One bold accent color draws attention to the key feature
- Colors should agree with the brand personality

## Viewing angle
- Front view, centered: maximum clarity
- Three-quarter view from above: shows depth and form; the usual choice for a hero image, and the right one for a low, flat product, which a front view reduces to its edge
- Close-up: shows a detail or a material
- Top-down: suits flat products and arranged sets
- In-context shot: shows the product in use

## Lighting
- Soft studio lighting: clean and professional, shows form and detail
- Side lighting: adds depth and dimension, emphasizes texture
- Backlight or rim light: dramatic silhouette, a premium feel
- Natural daylight: warm and approachable, suits lifestyle images

## Environment and background
- Isolated on a white or neutral background: all attention on the product; best for pitch decks
- Contextual: on a desk, in a hand, in a kitchen. It helps viewers imagine using the product.
- A context should support the product, never compete with it

## Composition
- Rule of thirds: place the product or its key feature on an intersection of a 3×3 grid
- Negative space: empty space gives breathing room, directs attention and conveys sophistication
- Depth: soft shadows suggest the product rests on a surface

## Mockups of digital products
- Show the interface on a real device at correct proportions
- High contrast and an obvious hierarchy
- Show one screen: the main use case, the "aha" moment, never the settings page
- Use believable content and data

## Common mockup mistakes
- Too much detail too soon: polishing before the concept is validated
- Unrealistic scenarios: data or settings nobody would believe
- Impossible features: showing what cannot be built
- Mixed styles in one image
- A sterile scene when the product's value lies in how it is used

## Words that mislead an image model
An image model draws the most common picture a word brings to mind. Choose words for the picture they produce.
- "A timer dial" on a kitchen scale produces a clock face. Write "a smooth rotating ring set flush in the surface".
- A product with a familiar shape keeps its name: a pen is "a pen", a bottle is "a bottle", a lamp is "a lamp".
- "A box", "a case" and "a device" name no particular shape, and the image model fills in the commonest one: a cube, a crate, a suitcase. Replace the word with the shape the product needs: "a low, flat rectangular container with rounded corners and a lid" for something that holds a flat object, "an upright cylinder with a cap" for something that holds a tall one.
- "Smart" and "high-tech" produce screens and blue glow. Name the one visible feature in their place.
- Describe what is there. An image model tends to draw whatever a prompt names, including what it says to leave out.

## Writing a mockup prompt for an image model
- Start with the shot type: "professional product photography of..."
- Describe the shape and its proportions, with a comparison of size
- Name the main material with its finish, then the accent if there is one
- Name the key feature and where it sits
- Add the one cue that shows what the product does
- State angle, lighting and background explicitly, one of each
- Put any visible text in double quotes, such as display reading "24°C", so the model renders it
- A vague prompt ("a nice lamp") gives a generic image; a specific one gives a usable mockup

## Three tests for a mockup
- Five-second test: after five seconds, can a viewer say what the product is, what it does and who it is for?
- Desire test: do they want to use it?
- Differentiation test: can they see how it differs from what exists?
