# Landing Page Principles
A landing page is the first page a visitor sees about a product. Its top part, the hero section, is what they see before scrolling. It has one job: to make the visitor understand the product and take one action. A landing page design is a picture of that hero section, made to show how the product would be presented, not to be built from.

## What the hero section must answer
A visitor decides in a few seconds whether to stay. In that time the hero section answers three questions, each with one element.
- What is it? The product photograph answers this.
- What does it do for me? The headline answers this.
- What do I do next? The button answers this.
Anything that answers none of the three is left out.

## The headline
The headline states the one outcome the customer gets, in their own words.
- At most five words; a headline that needs a comma is two headlines
- It names the outcome, not the product and not a feature: for a reading lamp that clips to a book, "Read without waking anyone", not "The clip-on LED lamp"
- Plain words, present tense, no superlatives: "best", "revolutionary" and "smart" say nothing
- It does not repeat the product name, which already stands in the logo
- If the outcome is unclear, the value proposition is unclear, and the headline cannot repair that

## The call to action
One button, with one action.
- Two words, verb first: "Order now", "Get yours", "Join waitlist"
- The label names what happens on the click; a product not yet for sale asks for a sign-up, not an order
- The button is the only element in the accent color, so the eye finds it
- A second button halves the attention each one gets

## Layout of a hero section
Two layouts cover almost every product.
- Split: logo, headline and button on the left, the product photograph on the right. It suits an upright or compact product.
- Centered: logo at the top, headline and button centered below it, the product photograph across the lower half. It suits a low, wide product.
The reading order is always logo, headline, button, product. Size sets the order: the headline is the largest element after the product, the logo the smallest.

## Visual style of the page
The page takes its style from the product, so the two read as one thing.
- Background: one calm, light color that contrasts with the product; a dark product on a warm off-white, a light product on a soft grey
- Text: one dark color for the headline
- Accent: one color, taken from the product or the logo, used only for the button
- Typography: one clean sans-serif typeface in two sizes
- Empty space around every element; a crowded page reads as cheap

## What an image model can draw on a page
An image model draws large lettering well and small lettering badly. The design is limited to what it can draw.
- Exactly two texts: the headline and the button label
- No tagline, no paragraph, no navigation menu, no footer: small text comes out as scribble
- Short words are drawn more reliably than long ones
- The logo and the product are not drawn again from words: the image model is shown the logo and the mockup made earlier, and places them on the page
- The prompt points to them ("the logo from the second image", "the black case from the first image") and does not describe them a second time

## Writing a landing page prompt for an image model
- Start with what the picture is: "landing page design for a product website, flat front view of the whole page"
- Name the background color
- Place the logo: "the logo from the second image at the top left"
- Give each of the two texts in double quotes, with its place and size: large headline reading "Read without waking anyone", a rounded button reading "Order now"
- Say where the product photograph sits and which image it comes from: "on the right a product photograph of the white lamp from the first image"
- End with the style: empty space, minimal modern web design, clean sans-serif typography
- Describe only what is on the page; an image model tends to draw whatever a prompt names, including what it says to leave out

## Common landing page mistakes
- A headline about the product and not about the customer
- Several messages competing in the first screen
- Two or more buttons
- A photograph that does not show what the product is
- Decoration that carries no information: gradients, icons, badges
- Colors on the page that fight with the product

## Three tests for a landing page
- Five-second test: after five seconds, can a visitor say what the product is and what it does for them?
- One-action test: is there exactly one thing to click?
- Squint test: with half-closed eyes, do the headline and the product still stand out?
