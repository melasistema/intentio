# Red Flags in Product Ideas
Warning signs that a product idea may struggle, fail or need a significant pivot. There are fifteen, grouped as problem, solution, market, business model and founder red flags. A single flag can often be overcome; several together are usually fatal.

## Problem red flag 1: The problem isn't painful enough
- Symptom: people acknowledge the problem but are not looking for a solution
- Why it fails: low pain means low willingness to change or pay
- Test: ask "What have you tried to solve this?" If the answer is "nothing", the pain is too low

## Problem red flag 2: The problem is rare
- Symptom: "this happened to me once"
- Why it fails: infrequent problems create little urgency and poor retention
- Threshold: at least weekly for consumer products, daily for B2B tools

## Problem red flag 3: The problem doesn't generalize
- Symptom: the founder is solving a personal problem and assumes others share it
- Why it fails: a hyper-specific problem means a tiny market
- Test: interview 20 people in the target segment; if fewer than 12 resonate strongly, rethink

## Problem red flag 4: Acceptable workarounds exist
- Symptom: "I just use [existing tool] for that"
- Why it fails: the solution must be 10x better to overcome switching costs
- Test: ask "What would make you switch?" A vague answer is a bad sign

## Solution red flag 5: A vitamin, not a painkiller
- Symptom: the product is nice to have rather than urgent
- Vitamins are preventative, optional and easy to forget; painkillers give immediate relief
- Why it fails: low urgency leads to low conversion and high churn
- Mitigation: build the product into a daily habit or an existing workflow

## Solution red flag 6: Heavy behavioral change required
- Symptom: users must change how they work before they see any value
- Why it fails: friction kills adoption
- Rule: if it takes more than three steps to show value, simplify

## Solution red flag 7: Solves only part of a workflow
- Symptom: users leave the product to finish the task somewhere else
- Why it fails: partial solutions add friction instead of removing it
- Test: map the full user journey; if the product covers less than 60% of it, widen the scope or integrate with the tools that cover the rest

## Market red flag 8: Undifferentiated in a crowded market
- Symptom: "it's like [successful product], but for [narrow niche]"
- Why it fails: without differentiation the fight is on price or marketing budget, which startups lose
- Test: complete "Unlike [competitor], we..."; if that is hard, the positioning is weak

## Market red flag 9: Too early or too late
- Too early: the idea depends on technology or habits most customers do not have yet, so customer education is expensive
- Too late: incumbents are entrenched
- Sweet spot: a growing market with weak incumbents

## Market red flag 10: Unclear target customer
- Symptom: "it's for everyone"
- Why it fails: no one can market, build or price for everyone
- Rule: if you cannot name 10 specific people who would use it tomorrow, the target is too broad

## Business model red flag 11: No clear path to revenue
- Symptom: "we'll figure out monetization later"
- Why it fails: monetization shapes product design and acquisition; it is not an afterthought
- Test: can you say today who pays, how much, and why?

## Business model red flag 12: Requires massive scale to work
- Symptom: "once we have a million users, the network effects kick in"
- Why it fails: most startups die before reaching scale
- Mitigation: design for value at 100 users

## Business model red flag 13: Assumes viral growth
- Symptom: "people will just share it"
- Why it fails: organic virality is rare; most products need paid acquisition
- Test: can customers be acquired profitably through paid channels?

## Founder red flag 14: Building for hype, not need
- Symptom: the pitch leads with a fashionable technology instead of a customer problem
- Fix: start from the problem, then choose the technology

## Founder red flag 15: Unwillingness to pivot
- Symptom: the idea is treated as untouchable
- Fix: commit to hypotheses, not solutions; test, learn, adapt

## When a red flag is acceptable
- Low frequency is acceptable when the pain is extreme, as with tax software used once a year
- A crowded market is acceptable with deep domain expertise or a unique distribution channel
- Behavioral change is acceptable when the benefit is massive
